Composite Top Shell & Field Repairs

Longhorn Racing Solar — Composites Team

Overview

The top shell is the largest composite part on the car: the aerodynamic skin that also carries the solar array. Junior year, the composites team rebuilt it from a heavy 6-ply layup into a 2-ply laminate stiffened by strategically placed bulkheads. I ran the ANSYS ACP analysis behind that change, prepared the tooling, laid up the shell, and wrote the documentation so the next team doesn't have to relearn it. At competition, when the bulkheads cracked, I designed the fix that kept us racing.

Laminate Design

The previous shell was a 6-ply carbon layup weighing over 100 lb. Laminate theory says most of that material wasn't doing anything — a thin skin is plenty for aero loads if it's held in shape. Composite FEA in ANSYS ACP let us iterate on ply schedule and bulkhead placement until a 2-ply skin with internal bulkheads met the stiffness targets at 20 lb. It was the team's best shell to date.

Tooling

A part this size needs a mold this size. We prepped a 5 × 16 ft EPS foam negative mold by sanding it smooth, skimming it with thin spackle, sanding, priming, sanding, applying gel coat, and sanding again — each pass chasing a tooling-quality surface that the carbon would pick up exactly. Any ridge in the mold becomes a ridge in the part.

Earlier, for smaller parts, I'd spent a week turning a salvaged steel sheet into a flat layup table: filling scratches with Bondo, then wet-sanding from 400 to 3000 grit. The thin sheet we'd been using warped under vacuum; the thicker one didn't.

Manufacturing

Vacuum-bag wet layup: release agent on the mold, plies cut at offset angles for multidirectional strength, resin and hardener mixed by a calculator we built to hit the right fiber-to-resin ratio by volume, each ply saturated with a squeegee, then peel ply, breather, tacky tape, bag, and vacuum for 24 hours. Respirators and gloves throughout.

I initiated the documentation from these layups — step by step, where things went wrong, and what to research or simplify next time. That process document now lives in the team database and is the baseline for the procedures I standardized as lead across 20+ carbon, Kevlar, fiberglass, and sandwich parts.

Competition Repair

During scrutineering, the middle rear of the shell started to sag. Some bulkheads had cracked and were no longer carrying load. We had roughly 24 hours before race day.

Laying up replacement bulkheads was impossible in the time — composite cure alone would blow the window. Steel braces would hold but add too much weight. The solution was sheet-metal doublers: thin metal plates bonded across each crack with high-performance structural epoxy, so load bypasses the split through the doubler. I also added pre-emptive doublers at the ribs showing early fatigue, since they'd been compensating for the broken ones. The shell held for the rest of competition.

Driver Egress Step

Regulations require the driver to be able to exit the car quickly, which meant a step the driver could stand on without deforming the shell. The step had to bond to the steel space frame and meet the compound curve of the shell's underside — and the CAD model couldn't be trusted for that curve, because hand layup stacks up too much tolerance.

I measured four corner heights and built up the curve numerically. The bulk of the height came from carbon sandwich panels stacked and bonded with EVA film, melted in an oven. The top layer that actually meets the shell is a 3D print in ASA at 3% hexagonal infill — strong in compression, fine in heat, and a fraction of the weight and lead time of machining a 3D curve into composite. It has survived 100+ exits and jumps, including the heaviest drivers, with no repairs.

Key Takeaways

  • Stiffness comes from geometry, not thickness — the bulkheads did more than four plies of carbon
  • Tooling surface quality is the ceiling on part quality; budget as much time for the mold as for the part
  • Document the layup while it's fresh, including what went wrong
  • Under time pressure, reroute the load instead of rebuilding the structure
  • When the CAD can't be trusted, measure the real part
