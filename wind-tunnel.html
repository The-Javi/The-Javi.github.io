Vortex Generators & Boundary Layer Control

Low-Speed Aerodynamics — Wind Tunnel Study

Overview

Investigated how vortex generator (VG) placement affects drag on a bluff-body vehicle. Wind tunnel testing and flow visualization were used to find the position that best delays boundary-layer separation.

Tools & Methods

  • Open-circuit wind tunnel testing
  • Flow visualization with smoke and surface tufts
  • Drag force measurement with a pistol-grip sensor
  • Drag coefficient (Cd) and power-loss analysis
  • Dimensional analysis using Reynolds number scaling

Experimental Setup

Tested a 1:12 scale stock car model across Reynolds numbers from 1.6 × 10⁵ to 6.6 × 10⁵ in five configurations:

  • No vortex generators (baseline)
  • Front placement (~5% of body length)
  • Mid placement (~50%)
  • Rear placement (~80%)
  • Optimized placement (~65%)

Key Engineering Insights

Flow Behavior

Vortex generators energize the boundary layer, delaying separation and shrinking the wake. Flow visualization showed that well-placed VGs kept the flow attached further along the body.

Placement Sensitivity

  • Front placement: increased drag through early turbulence and higher skin friction
  • Mid placement: minimal or inconsistent effect
  • Rear-mid (~65%): most effective — delayed separation and reduced the wake

Results

Drag Reduction

The optimized placement reduced drag by roughly 3% relative to the baseline across the tested Reynolds numbers.

Power Impact

The drag reduction was measurable, but the corresponding power savings were minimal at this scale — a reminder that Reynolds-number mismatch limits how far model results extrapolate to a full-size vehicle.

Key Takeaways

  • Small geometric changes can significantly affect boundary-layer behavior
  • VG placement matters more than whether VGs are present at all
  • Flow visualization is essential for interpreting aerodynamic results
  • Scale testing has real limits; know what your Reynolds number does and doesn't tell you
