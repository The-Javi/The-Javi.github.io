Battery Enclosure Latch

Longhorn Racing Solar — Enclosures Subsystem, 2023–24

Overview

My first year on the team was on Enclosures: the subsystem that designs the housings for the car's electronics and its battery box, with two semesters to design, test, and manufacture everything. My main contribution was the lock that holds the battery enclosure shut — the design that raced at competition.

Requirements

  • Lightweight and easy to manufacture
  • Fit within a 1-inch clearance space
  • Withstand crash loading from the full battery pack
  • Interface with a Kevlar composite panel

First Iteration

The first design took about a month and was too complicated: too many bolts, too many unique parts, and a hard interface with the car's frame. My system lead reviewed it with me and the criticism was fair. It went back to the drawing board.

Second Iteration

The second design borrowed the mechanism a door latch uses and actuated it with cables, so it could be released without reaching into the enclosure. I ran FEA to pick material and thickness, looked up materials and prices to keep cost down, used standard hardware wherever possible, and 3D-printed a prototype to prove the mechanism. Working prototype in two weeks; slim, few parts, and it's what went on the car.

What I Learned on Enclosures

This was where I learned to CAD the way industry does. We were taught top-down (master, or skeleton) modeling at the start of the year — confusing at first, and now the only way I'll build an assembly. Every enclosure was driven from a shared master sketch so parts stayed coordinated as the design changed. We also used a shared CAD database (BILD) with a check-out system, so only one person could edit a file at a time and mistakes could be rolled back.

Five people in the subsystem, 9-to-5 workdays every Saturday, weekly subsystem meetings, and biweekly system meetings. A good year.

Key Takeaways

  • The first design that works on paper is usually too complicated; the second one is the real one
  • Standard parts beat custom parts unless there's a reason
  • A printed prototype settles arguments faster than a CAD review
  • Top-down modeling is worth the learning curve
