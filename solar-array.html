Solar Array Manufacturing

Longhorn Racing Solar — Array Lead, Summer 2026

Overview

Two months before competition, the car's solar array was 48 cells into a 297-cell build and nobody was available to finish it. I took over the manufacturing schedule and the integration onto the top shell — and first had to fix the process, because at the rate we were laminating we weren't going to make it.

Starting Point

Two years earlier the team had a decent lamination process; that knowledge had left with the people who had it. The restarted effort was producing modules in over an hour each, with visible and tangible ridges and air bubbles in the surface. Lumpy encapsulation costs you power and looks bad on a car whose whole top surface is the array.

Process Development

Through a series of empirical tests, I got lamination down to about 30 minutes per module and the surface to a mirror finish. The changes that mattered:

  • Flipped the cells sunny-side down so the finished face forms against the smooth tool surface instead of the vacuum bag.
  • Started the oven at melt temperature rather than ramping up, soaking, and cooling — the ramp wasn't doing anything useful.
  • Staged the vacuum pressure with a regulator: lower pressure first to let air bubbles escape, then full pressure to consolidate.
  • Slowed the cooldown from fans and heat sinks to semi-slow, so cells cool uniformly without dimples and indents.
  • Pre-cut everything into an assembly line so lamination was the only thing happening at the laminator.

I also reached out to industry contacts to understand how production lamination is normally done and where our process diverged. Output went from 7 modules a week to 4 a day, which is what put the timeline back inside the competition date.

Integration on a Conductive Shell

Every module has exposed leads that connect to its neighbors. For aerodynamics and a clean look, the leads drop through holes in the top shell to connect underneath — but the shell is carbon fiber, and carbon fiber conducts. Each lead had to be insulated where it passed through while staying live.

Commercial grommets ran about $3 for five and we needed 100. I designed and 3D-printed our own, sized exactly to the leads, for effectively no material cost. For waterproofing, I developed a potting method from underneath: the printed grommet, a short plastic tube, and RTV silicone form a "top hat" around each lead that seals the hole and strain-relieves the connection so it can't be pulled out.

Validation

Every module's I-V curve was measured and checked against spec before it went on the shell, so a bad cell or a bad lamination was caught at the bench rather than on the car.

Key Takeaways

  • Process knowledge walks out the door unless it's written down — the first job was recovering what the team already knew once
  • Small sequencing changes (orientation, pressure staging, cooldown rate) were worth more than any new equipment
  • Throughput, not cycle time, is the number that matters against a deadline
  • When the off-the-shelf part doesn't fit the budget or the geometry, print it
